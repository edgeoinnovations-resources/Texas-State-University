What this measures

Knowing a subject isn't the same as being disposed to teach it a certain way. The Teaching Spatial Thinking through Geography Disposition Inventory (Jo & Bednarz, 2014) captures teachers' attitudes, values, and beliefs across 40 items in five subcategories, each rated 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Here it's applied cross-nationally. The headline: both groups sit in the low-to-moderate range, and Chinese pre-service teachers scored higher in all five subcategories — most sharply on their willingness to explicitly teach spatial concepts.

A quick orientation to Cohen's d (effect size): ~0.2 is small, ~0.5 medium, ~0.8 large. With samples this big, almost any gap is "statistically significant," so d is the better guide to what actually matters.

Who scored how — drill-down

The striking non-finding: dispositions barely moved with gender, grade level, or number of geography courses taken. Where the action is, is between universities — and one Korean outlier tells a story.

5

Honesty notes

Every number is from the paper

All item means, subcategory means, effect sizes, and university figures are reproduced from Tables 2–5. Nothing here is illustrative — the inventory items are the study's real wording. One caution about the source: the article's text names Shandong Normal's mean as "3.53", but its own Table 4 lists 3.93 (and calls Shandong the highest, which only 3.93 supports) — this page uses the Table 4 value.

Read the scores conservatively

The authors flag moderate response bias — respondents cluster near the neutral midpoint, so means of 3.0–4.0 shouldn't be read as strong positive dispositions, and the 4.0 "positive" threshold is theoretical, not empirical. The reverse-scored items (Q4, Q11, Q15, Q16, Q34) are worded negatively on purpose. And this is a snapshot of two countries at one time — it describes current conditions, not a universal ranking. The authors' takeaway is developmental: positive dispositions don't appear automatically with age or coursework; they need a deliberate spatial-thinking curriculum in teacher preparation.
