What this instrument is

Taking a quiz or test is a "convergent knowledge expression" activity — students demonstrate structured prior knowledge. It's the most direct way to prepare for the APHG exam, where scoring a 3+ (on 1–5) earns college credit. This paper documents two technology-based prep workflows and how each is used to gauge understanding, find gaps, and plan teaching.

Two exam sections

60 + 3

60 multiple-choice questions and 3 free-response questions — each section weighted equally.

MCQ pace

1 min

60 MCQs in 60 minutes — one minute per question. Time pressure is itself a skill to practice.

To earn credit

3+

A score of 3 or higher on the 1–5 scale earns college credit — the students' main motivation.

Workflow 1 · MCQ & FRQ

AP Classroom — Personal Progress Checks

College Board's online platform. Formative MCQs (auto-graded, with answer explanations) and FRQs (with rubrics for self-scoring) for each unit. Used here as pre- and post-tests, followed by a structured self-reflection.

Workflow 2 · FRQ

Google Forms — FRQ build & grade loop

A familiar, low-friction tool for authoring FRQs with hidden per-question feedback and a timer, collecting typed responses, letting students self-score against the rubric, then grading anonymized answers question-by-question.

Personal Progress Check & self-reflection

PPCs report a student's score as a number and on a gray → yellow → green color scheme. Used as pre/post-tests, they let students watch their mastery move from gray toward green. Drag the sliders to set a pre- and post-test score, then work through the self-reflection prompts. (The point is learning, not grading — teachers check completion, not scores.)

Color bands are illustrative cutoffs: gray = low, yellow = middle, green = high. The paper describes the gray→yellow→green scheme but does not publish exact thresholds.

Anatomy of an FRQ — the five task verbs

Since 2019, every APHG free-response question is worth exactly seven points, and its parts are phrased with five standardized task verbs. Click each verb to see what it asks students to do, with an illustrative prompt.

Pick a task verb above.

Why the seven-point rule matters here: the 2019 standardization lets teachers update older FRQs (which had varying point totals) into fresh seven-point questions — a practical way to build new, copyright-safe practice items while staying aligned to the exam.

The four-step FRQ build & grade loop

Google Forms turns one FRQ into a full cycle. Toggle between the teacher's and the student's side, then click through the four steps. Steps 2–4 include working tools: a 25-minute timer, a self-scoring rubric, and anonymized question-by-question grading.

Honesty notes

Illustrative items, faithful structure

The two figures in the paper are images not reproduced in its text, so two things here are original illustrations: the seven self-reflection prompts (Fig. 1) and the sample FRQ, its rubric, and the sample student answers (Fig. 2). They are built to match the workflows the paper describes. Everything structural is faithful: two sections of 60 MCQ + 3 FRQ, the pre/post-test-plus-reflection PPC routine, the gray→yellow→green scheme, the seven-point FRQ standardization, the five task verbs (compare, define, describe, explain, identify), the 25-minute FRQ timing, and the four Google Forms steps.

A practitioner article, and one caution the authors raise

This is a methods paper with no sample, experiment, or outcome statistics — its evidence is teacher experience. The authors themselves flag that AP Classroom has real technical friction (repeated log-ins, an unintuitive interface) and that these "may not be one-size-fits-all methods." They also stress the PPC routine should not be graded — its purpose is reflection, and removing grade pressure reduces cheating.
