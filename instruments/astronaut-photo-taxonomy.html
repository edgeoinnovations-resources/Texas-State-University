Astronaut-Photo Taxonomy Variant

Can photos taken by astronauts on the Space Station teach students to think spatially? This study handed undergraduates six real ISS photographs to geolocate — coding the spatial concepts they used against Jo's taxonomy, extended for remote sensing. 1

What this is

Over 1.5 million astronaut photographs of Earth are free to the public through NASA's Gateway to Astronaut Photography of Earth. In a "Principles of Remote Sensing" lab, 32 undergraduates were given six uncataloged ISS photos and asked to geolocate each one in Google Earth — starting from only the ISS nadir point (the lat/long directly below the station when the shot was taken) and their own reading of the landscape. Their written interpretations were coded against the 2009 Taxonomy of Spatial Thinking (Jo & Bednarz), extended with remote-sensing primitives. Repeating the task three times across the semester let the authors chi-square-test whether spatial thinking improved.

The taxonomy — and what got added

Jo & Bednarz (2009) sort spatial concepts into three levels of increasing sophistication. This study adopted them as-is and added four primitives (highlighted) that matter for reading satellite imagery.

What the data showed

Across all 18 photo-assignments, the chi-square tests told a consistent story — and a useful one for teaching.

Primitives win

In every one of the 18 assignments, students used significantly more primitive concepts than simple or complex ones — so all six photos are reliable for teaching basic spatial concepts.

3 of 6

Photos 2, 4, and 5 produced a statistically significant shift toward higher-level concepts from assignment 1 to 2 — real improvement. Photos 1, 3, and 6 didn't move.

Stop at 2

The third assignment never differed significantly from the second — so the authors recommend just two assignments per semester (the third-round drop-off is likely final-exam fatigue).

The bigger takeaway: match the photo to the concept. A photo's spatial richness determines what it can teach — the distinct lake color for “Color,” the Richat rings for “Pattern/Shape,” Bermuda's blue tones for “Gradient,” Misrata's streets for “Network.”

5

Honesty notes

Schematic images, real data

The six visuals are original schematic illustrations, not the NASA photographs — the real images (public-domain, courtesy NASA JSC's Earth Science & Remote Sensing Unit) are linked in the sources by their ISS photo IDs. Every concept percentage and chi-square verdict is reproduced from the paper's Table 2 and Appendices. One source quirk: the paper's photo-ID footnote lists ISS044-E-8687 for both photo 2 and photo 6, which is almost certainly a typo for photo 6.

A modest, honest study

This is a single course of 32 students (of 54 enrolled; 22 were dropped for copying prior answers into the third round). It's the taxonomy applied in a new setting rather than a controlled experiment, and the authors are candid that improvement showed in only half the photos and that gains were small. The value is practical: a worked method — and a starter data set — for choosing astronaut photos to teach specific spatial concepts. The taxonomy's three levels are Jo & Bednarz's (2009); this study's contribution is the remote-sensing adaptation and the photo-to-concept guide.
