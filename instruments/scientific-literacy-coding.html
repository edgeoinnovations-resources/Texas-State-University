Can a geography curriculum teach scientific literacy? They coded it to find out.

This study built a four-domain scientific-literacy framework and used it to code all 100 learning objectives in China’s national middle-school geography curriculum. Below, you can become the coder — tag real objectives yourself, then see how the researchers scored them and where the curriculum’s strengths and blind spots lie.

The coding scheme: four domains, nine codes

Scientific literacy was split into four domains and nine leaf-codes (only the Inquiry domain subdivides, into B-1…B-4). Click a code to read its definition and how often it turned up. The bar under each is its share of the 100 objectives.

Tip: the two dashed bars are codes that appeared in zero objectives — the curriculum’s blind spots.

Here are four real objectives from the curriculum, in ascending complexity. Pick every code you think applies, then reveal how the researchers coded it. Watch the codes stack up — and feel the study’s hardest call: describing data (B-2) vs. explaining it (B-3).

What the curriculum actually emphasizes

Across all 100 objectives, the codes fell wildly unevenly. Geography leans hard on knowledge and inquiry — especially interpreting data (map reading) and reasoning — but several codes barely register, and two never appear at all.

The blind spots. Two codes scored zero: B-4 (evaluating scientific inquiry) and F (fostering interest in science). And despite the curriculum’s stated devotion to everyday life, personal, everyday use of science (D) was just 2% — the authors call this “surprising.” Science-related ethics (E) was also 2%. A curriculum can be rich in reasoning yet still leave students never asked to evaluate, apply, or care.
