The Spatial Thinking Ability Test (STAT)

A standardized, 16-item test that scores how well a person reasons with maps, space, and geographic representations. Jo didn't build this one — she reused it as the pre/post yardstick in her Web-based GIS experiment.

↻  Reused instrument — built by Lee & Bednarz (2012), applied by Jo, Hong & Verma (2016)
16 multiple-choice items
8 types (I–VIII)
0–16 score range
2 equivalent forms (pre/post)

1What the STAT is — and why Jo reached for it

Most of Jo's flagship instruments are ones she built herself (the Taxonomy, the Disposition Inventory). The STAT is the clearest example of the opposite habit — a mark of methodological maturity that the toolkit calls out directly: she doesn't reinvent a good measure.

A standardized outcome measure

The STAT integrates geographic content knowledge with spatial-thinking skills into one scored test. Because it was "developed through rigorous validity and reliability tests," it's built for replication across different populations and settings — exactly what a quasi-experiment needs on both ends of an intervention.

Two equivalent forms

The STAT ships as two parallel forms so students can be tested before and after a course without simply memorizing answers. Each form has 16 multiple-choice items covering the same eight types, plus three demographic questions (gender, major, prior geospatial coursework). No time limit — most finish in about 20 minutes.

Lineage: SST → STAT

The STAT (Lee & Bednarz, 2012) is a revision and expansion of the earlier Spatial Skills Test (SST) from Lee & Bednarz (2009). The 2009 SST focused narrowly on understanding spatial-relation concepts; the 2012 STAT widened the lens to include geographic content and skills that support spatial thinking in real geographic contexts. When you cite "the STAT," you mean the 2012 paper.

3How Jo used it: the 2016 Web-GIS experiment

Jo, Hong & Verma (2016) ran a non-equivalent control group quasi-experiment: 306 undergraduates across 7 world-geography sections at two universities. Two sections did Web-based GIS (ArcGIS Online) activities; five didn't. Everyone took the STAT before and after the semester. Scores were compared within each class using paired t-tests.

The result

Only the two Web-GIS sections improved significantly: Class 2b +1.17 points (p<.01, d=.51, medium) and Class 3 +1.60 points (p=.01, d=.74, medium). None of the five control sections showed a statistically significant gain. On a 0–16 scale these are modest but real shifts — and they land exactly where the intervention should push.

Which skills moved?

Breaking the gains down by item type is where the STAT earns its keep. Of the eight types, only three improved significantly in one or more classes — and they're the ones GIS work actually exercises.

4Where the STAT sits in her program

The toolkit's throughline is instrument continuity. The STAT is the outside measure she plugs in when she needs a standardized spatial-thinking outcome she didn't have to build and validate herself.

2009 Lee & Bednarz publish the SST (spatial skills test) — focused on spatial-relation concepts.
2012 Lee & Bednarz expand it into the STAT: 16 items, 8 types, validated by factor analysis on 532 students.
2016 Jo, Hong & Verma reuse the STAT as the pre/post outcome in the Web-GIS quasi-experiment (this study).
2015 · 2018 Jo references the STAT in her agenda chapter and the secondary-geography research summary, flagging it as a validated but underutilized tool the field should use more.

5Read it critically

Theory vs. factor structure

In the original 2012 paper, the spatial components that emerged from factor analysis only partly coincided with the spatial concepts used to write the questions — and differed from components other researchers had hypothesized. The eight "types" are a design scaffold, not eight cleanly separable, empirically confirmed factors.

No reliability figure in Jo's paper

The 2016 study calls the STAT rigorously validated but reports no specific reliability coefficient (no Cronbach's alpha) for its own administration. If you cite a reliability value, get it from the 2012 source, not the 2016 application.

Quasi-experiment, not a trial

No randomization. Intact class sections were compared as-is with convenience sampling, so pre-existing group differences can't be ruled out — the authors say so plainly. The pre-test means already differ across sections (7.4 to 10.1).

The STAT doesn't measure everything

Jo notes the STAT "does not cover all content and skills taught in a variety of areas in geography." It's a focused spatial-thinking measure — useful precisely because it's narrow, not a general geography exam.
