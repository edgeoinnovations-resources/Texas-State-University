Lesson-Plan Spatiality: Pre/Post Comparator

Does teaching teachers about spatial thinking actually change how they plan lessons? This study coded 24 pre-service teachers' lesson plans — before and after a spatial-thinking workshop — using the 2009 Taxonomy. Code a few objectives yourself, then run the workshop and watch the numbers move.

What this measures

Pedagogical content knowledge (PCK) is a teacher's ability to make a subject teachable. Here it's operationalized concretely: the "spatiality" of the lesson plans they write. Twenty-four pre-service teachers wrote a lesson plan on Canada's settlement patterns, attended a ~4-hour workshop on teaching spatial thinking, then wrote another. Every objective and assessment item in both plans was coded on the three dimensions of the 2009 Taxonomy of Spatial Thinking — the instrument you've already explored in this Lab.

A lesson plan asked for 3 objectives + step-by-step procedures + 3 assessment items. 71 objectives & 71 items (pre) and 71 objectives & 74 items (post) were coded.

How to read the cognitive shift: a drop in input-level and a rise in processing- and output-level is the desired direction — it means objectives moved from mere recall toward analysis and creation. The clearest single gain: complex-spatial concepts in objectives nearly doubled (15.5% → 26.8%).
4

What teachers actually changed

Beyond the numbers, the qualitative analysis found four concrete strategies pre-service teachers adopted in their revised plans.

Strategy 1

Teach concepts explicitly

Set aside time to define spatial concepts directly instead of assuming students know them — “explicitly teach about what a region is.”

Strategy 2

Use richer spatial concepts

Overlay, pattern, and distribution appeared far more often — e.g., overlaying population density with terrain, weather, and resources.

Strategy 3

Put representations in students' hands

Not just showing maps, but having students create their own charts, graphs, and diagrams to portray information.

Strategy 4

Push toward higher-order thinking

Ask students to hypothesize, explain their reasoning, and justify decisions rather than just name and list.

“What I really got from the workshop was not just assuming that my students know what these things are but explicitly describing them.” — a participant, on revising from pre- to post-lesson plan

Honesty notes

Every number is from the paper — a couple of items are illustrative

All pre/post percentages are reproduced from Figure 2 and the text. The three lettered coding items in Section 2 are the paper's actual Table 1 examples; items 4–5 are original illustrations built from the concepts the study discusses (overlay, distribution, hypothesizing). One assessment figure — processing-level, pre — isn't stated directly and is inferred as the remainder so the three cognitive levels sum to 100%; it's flagged in the chart.

A small, short, single-group study

This is 24 pre-service teachers, one ~4-hour workshop, with no control group and pre/post plans on the same topic — so the gains are encouraging but not proof of lasting classroom change (the authors call for longitudinal follow-up). Note the honest limit the authors stress themselves: even after the workshop, about 70% of objectives and over half of assessment items still used no spatial representation — the hardest skill to build. Percentage drops in primitives/simple-spatial reflect a shift of emphasis toward complex-spatial, not a devaluing, since each plan allowed only three objectives.
