What this instrument is

Students are "digital natives" who reach for the web first — but using online information well means being able to find, evaluate, and select it. This case study looked at exactly that: what source types undergraduates in a world geography course actually chose for an assignment, and what criteria they used to decide a source was useful. The task centered on a real global issue — recent changes in oil prices — tied to the North Africa & Southwest Asia unit.

The 13 source types

Every source was classified into one of 13 types. Toggle between how often each type was used and how useful students judged it to be — then click any bar for examples. The twist: the type students used most is not the one they rated most useful.

3

The seven usefulness criteria

Students tagged each source with reasons from seven paired criteria — a useful quality and its exact opposite. Definitions were adopted from Lee, Paik & Jo (2012); the "not useful" mirror-reasons were added for this study.

Which reasons actually drove the rankings?

Reasons students gave for their five most-useful vs five least-useful sources (n = 325 each). Comprehensiveness made a source useful; limited detail made it useless.

Percentages exceed neither 100% nor sum tidily — students could give more than one reason per source.

Honesty notes

Real data, illustrative sample sources

Every frequency, percentage, and usefulness ranking here is reproduced from the article's Tables 1–3. The six sources in the "Try it" section are original illustrations written to represent each type — the study did not publish its actual source list. The criteria definitions are the study's own wording, adopted from Lee, Paik & Jo (2012).

What the study can and can't tell us

This is a single case study of 65 students on one topic, so the authors caution against over-generalizing. Students were asked only to select reasons, not explain them, so the "why" behind each judgment wasn't captured. Note the one true outlier: online discussion forums show a strong average rank of 9/20, but that's based on a single source out of 1,300 — not a reliable signal. The headline finding is sobering: students prioritized comprehensiveness and ease of understanding over credibility, judging sources mostly on surface features rather than actual content.
