How rigorous is geography-education research? They coded 191 studies to find out.

To audit the health of the subfield, this team built a coded database of every empirical study in three geography-education journals — classifying each by design, sample, technique, and statistics. The picture is revealing: lots of variety, but a shared reporting weakness that undermines replication.

The corpus

Every empirical article in three long-running geography-education journals. They found 197; six were discarded for unclear design, leaving 191. Counts differ by each journal's scope and publication frequency.

What designs does the field use?

Each study was coded quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods from its methods section. Mixed methods is the most common — click a bar to drill into its techniques and example studies.

The rigor gap

The audit's sharpest finding lives in the 55 quantitative studies. Nearly all ran statistical tests — but almost none reported the practical significance (effect size) or the precision (confidence interval) that make results replicable and comparable.

Reporting practices among the 55 quantitative studies
“No single study reported both together.” The p-value was almost the only indicator of significance. Only 8 studies reported an effect size and only 4 a confidence interval — and not one reported both, even though the APA had recommended both since 2009. This is exactly the reporting habit that blocks replication and meta-analysis.
And the qualitative side fragments. The 65 qualitative studies used 26 slightly different data-collection approaches — so many that, in the authors' words, “there was not a single pair of articles of which the results are comparable.” Variety is healthy; incomparability is not.

The fixes the authors prescribe

1

Report practical significance. Pair every p-value with an effect size and a confidence interval, plus basic descriptive stats, so studies can be replicated.

2

Coordinate qualitative work. Replicate the same questions and methods with different participants and settings — reporting procedures in enough detail to make that possible.

3

Fully specify mixed-methods designs. State the data-collection methods, analysis, timing/sequence, and the purpose of mixing — most articles left this to the reader to guess.

4

Build cumulatively. Use exemplars and prior work to produce longitudinal, comparable research — “so that in ten years we are not repeating ourselves about the need for more data.”
