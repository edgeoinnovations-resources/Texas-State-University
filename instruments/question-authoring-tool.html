Why not just Bloom's Taxonomy?

Questions are a teacher's most everyday tool — but not all questions make students think, and fewer make them think spatially. Bloom's Taxonomy helps push questions toward higher-order thinking, but it's blind to two of the three pillars of spatial thinking: knowing spatial concepts and using tools of representation. The 2009 Taxonomy of Spatial Thinking fixes that by scoring a question on all three components at once, and it's the engine behind the tool below.

2

Score a question

Type a question (or load an example), then code it on the three dimensions. The tool computes its cell (1–24), checks which spatial-thinking components it engages, and suggests how to strengthen it.

3

The 24 cells

Every question lands in one of 24 cells — 4 concept levels × 2 representation options × 3 cognitive levels. The six green cells engage all three components of spatial thinking (a spatial concept + a representation + reasoning-level cognition). Your current question is outlined.

Also a design tool — matched pre/post questions

Beyond scoring existing questions, the taxonomy helps design new ones of equal difficulty. In one author's pre-test/post-test study on central place theory — comparing dynamic GIS maps against paper maps — questions were deliberately clustered in the hardest cells so that matched pre- and post-questions shared a cell number without simply repeating, and to avoid ceiling effects (perfect pre-test scores that could only fall).

Questions were clustered among these high-difficulty cells (complex-spatial concept + a map):

Complex-spatial · uses a map · output-level

Same cell number → comparable content, representation, and difficulty, without asking the identical question twice.

Honesty notes

The cell numbers are exact; a couple of examples are paraphrased

The 24-cell numbering here is reconstructed from the paper's own four worked examples (Cell 1, 19, 10, 24) and reproduces them exactly. The example questions "Define natural vegetation" and "Define population density" are the paper's; the map-based examples paraphrase the Figure 1 items the text describes (location on a map → Cell 10; distribution on a map → Cell 24), since the figure's exact wording isn't in the article text. The "fully-integrated" (green) set follows the paper's stated principle — a spatial concept + a representation + reasoning-level (processing/output) cognition — as Figure 2's exact coloring isn't reproduced here.

A tool, honestly scoped

Coding a real question still takes judgment — a question can touch more than one concept, and "which cognitive level" depends on what students have already been taught (recalling a definition is input; deriving it fresh is higher). The taxonomy is a lens for deliberate design, not an automatic grader. And gray cells aren't "bad" — recall and single-component questions are necessary scaffolding; the point is to make sure students also meet fully-integrated spatial questions.
