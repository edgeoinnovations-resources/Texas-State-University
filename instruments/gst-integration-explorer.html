GST Integration-Level Explorer

Using GPS, remote sensing, and GIS together isn't all-or-nothing — technologies can be woven in at four increasing depths. This model, at the heart of Jo & Muñiz Solari's agenda for geospatial education, maps those four levels. Flip between them and watch how data flows differently at each.

1

The three technologies

Geospatial technologies (GST) are "an integrated body of technologies." Three do the heavy lifting — and how tightly they're woven together defines the depth of geospatial practice. The chapter adopts Gao's (2002) idea that there are four possible levels of integrating them.

GPS
Global Positioning System

Locates and records precise positions in the field.

RS
Remote Sensing

Satellite/aerial imagery, analyzed by segmentation and classification.

GIS
Geographic Information System

Stores, layers, analyzes, and visualizes spatial data.

2

Four levels of integration

Pick a level to see how data moves between GPS, RS, and GIS — from a one-way chain that ends at GIS, to full many-directional exchange. Each level comes with a classroom example.

3

Where the model sits — the agenda's two pillars

The integration levels are one piece of a bigger picture. Jo & Muñiz Solari propose a curricular model for "integral geography education with GST" resting on two pillars — and the four-level model lives inside the second.

Pillar A

Spatial abilities

  • Spatial literacy — understanding spatial concepts, from basic (location, direction, distance) to advanced (nodes, network, diffusion).
  • Spatial thinking across three orders — practicing in space (personal), about space (physical), and with space (abstract).
  • Built on three components: concepts of space, tools of representation, and processes of reasoning.
Pillar B

Geospatial integrative skills

  • Data handling — processing, integration, preservation, and accessibility across the geospatial data life cycle.
  • Data sharing — open sharing, metadata, and the laws governing data use.
  • Technology integration — weaving GPS, RS, and GIS together — this is where the four-level model applies.
The design principles: instruction and assessment should be built around spatial thinking and geographic inquiry. GST is powerful but not a panacea — it helps most when lessons stress understanding concepts and applying them, not just acquiring facts. This two-pillar agenda is Jo & Muñiz Solari's own contribution; the four-level integration model within it is Gao's.
4

Honesty notes

Levels 1–2 are detailed; levels 3–4 are named

The chapter describes the Linear and Interactive levels in detail, including the exact data-flow logic and the classroom examples reproduced here (minimal GIS; the GPS → georeference → RS → GIS → modeling loop). It names Hierarchical and Complex as Gao's higher levels — "common when students in higher education develop GP" — but doesn't elaborate them. So those two descriptions and their examples (marked illustrative) reconstruct Gao's model logic rather than quote the chapter.

A reused model inside an original agenda

The four-level integration model is Gao's (2002) — Fig. 17.2 is "adapted from Gao 2002" — which is why this page is marked reused (blue). The surrounding curricular agenda (the two pillars, the design principles) is Jo & Muñiz Solari's own framework. The data-flow diagram is a schematic of the logic of each level, not a literal redraw of the book figure. The authors' recommendation is explicit: aim for the Interactive level in school curricula as a "reasonable integration."
