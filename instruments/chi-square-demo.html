What the test does

In the astronaut-photo study, every spatial concept a student used was coded as a primitive, simple, or complex concept (using Jo's taxonomy). A natural question: did students use the three levels equally, or did they lean heavily on one? The chi-square goodness-of-fit test answers exactly that — it measures how far the observed counts stray from what you'd expect if all three were used equally (33.3% each). A big gap yields a big χ²; if χ² clears the critical value (5.991 for df = 2), the difference is statistically significant. For df = 2 the p-value has a tidy exact form: p = e^(−χ²/2).

Load the real counts from any photo and assignment (or type your own), and watch each term (O−E)²/E build toward the total. The null hypothesis: primitive, simple, and complex concepts were each used a third of the time.

Did spatial thinking change over the semester?

A second use of the test: instead of an equal-use null, compare assignment 2 to the distribution from assignment 1. If χ² clears 5.991, the mix of concepts shifted significantly — evidence that students' spatial thinking moved. Below, each photo's assignment-1 → assignment-2 test is computed live from the real counts.

Honesty notes

The math is the paper's — computed live, with two source typos flagged

Every count is from the study's Table 2, and the even-split test (Section 2) reproduces the paper's chi-square values exactly — Photo 1 / Assignment 1 = 48.55, Photo 5 / Assignment 1 = 98.96, matching Appendix I. Two source quirks worth flagging: (1) the worked example (Table 3a) lists the primitive term as 24.5744, a typo for 27.5744 — only the corrected value sums to the paper's own reported total of 48.5539; and (2) in the progression test (Section 3), the paper's Appendix V reports 140.23 for Photo 5, which is inconsistent with its own documented method (Table 4) and the raw counts, both of which give ≈6.12 — almost certainly a typo. This tool uses the correct arithmetic throughout; reassuringly, the significant / not-significant decision for all six photos matches the paper regardless. The p-values use the exact df = 2 formula e^(−χ²/2).

A standard test, and what it can't tell you

Chi-square goodness-of-fit is a general statistical method, not Jo's own instrument — which is why this page is marked as an applied technique (blue) rather than a built one. It tells you whether a distribution differs from expectation, not why, and it's sensitive to sample size (big samples make small differences "significant"). A significant result on "A2 vs A1" means the concept mix shifted — the study reads that as improved spatial thinking, but that interpretation rests on the coding and the study design, not the test alone. The concept coding it operates on comes from Jo & Bednarz's taxonomy — the built instrument elsewhere in this Lab.
