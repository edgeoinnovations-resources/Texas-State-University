What this instrument is

APHG is a content-heavy course — seven units across roughly 130 class periods — leaving little room for projects mid-year. But once the exam ends in early May, engagement is hard to hold. This project-based-learning (PBL) capstone, refined by a group of teachers over seven years, fills those final weeks by asking groups to apply everything they learned. Students know zombies aren't real; the pop-culture frame is just the hook for authentic geographic problem-solving.

Component 1

Research

Investigate the physical environment, population, agriculture, economy, and resources of places around the world.

Component 2

Route & migration

Determine and map the best migration route to survive a hypothetical outbreak and reach an infection-free zone.

Component 3

Shelter model

Build a 3-D model of a sustainable, long-term settlement at the route's final destination.

Honesty notes

Faithful protocol, illustrative options

The day structure, tasks, guiding questions, three disaster types, deliverables, group size, five-minute presentations, and the unit mappings are reproduced faithfully from the article. The starting regions and the biome resource details in the simulator are original illustrations — the paper's regional maps (Fig. 1) and example project photos (Figs. 2–5) are images not reproduced in its text. The government, economic, cultural, and sustainability options are illustrative choices that fit the categories the paper names (e.g., economic systems: traditional, market, socialist, communist).

Keep the geography, not the zombies

The authors are emphatic: students' research focus should not be on zombies. Although the frame is hypothetical, students should use reliable information and stay realistic wherever applicable. This is a practitioner article — refined over seven years and rich in classroom tips — but it reports teacher experience, not an experiment or outcome statistics. Full student instructions, maps, and the grading rubric live at the authors' project link.
